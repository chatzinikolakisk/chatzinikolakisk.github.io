I let out an audible groan on Slack when I found out what our new VP of Engineering wanted from me in his first week.

The org chart was still fresh. His request was direct and completely reasonable: he wanted delivery confidence. Evidence that my three teams were moving, shipping, building. A clear signal he could read and carry upwards.

My private reaction was somewhere between surprise and mild irritation. My teams had been running their own ceremonies without me, shipping to production without me in the room, escalating the right edge cases, and resolving the rest on their own. The delivery engine was working.

What I didn't have, apparently, was a way to make a quiet engine visible to someone who hadn't been watching it get built.

## Designed absence, not accidental absence

Earlier this year I wrote about taking an unexpected day off and watching my leads step into the gap. That lesson was accidental: I disappeared for a day, and people rose to the occasion.

Over the summer, the absences stopped being accidental. They became designed.

One of my leads runs a cross-team QA chapter sync. For months, my attendance meant "this matters to the director." Eventually it started meaning something worse: that she needed supervision. So after spending weeks aligning with her on what decisions the chapter should surface and what good looked like, I declined the recurring invite. I wanted the room looking at her, not at me.

Around the same time, another lead came to me with his nine-day August leave plan. He wasn't just naming a backup to keep the lights on. He had spent months giving a senior engineer on his squad gradually larger pieces of product and stakeholder ownership, and he was using his holiday to hand her the steering wheel completely: product meetings, stakeholder calls, daily trade-offs.

There is a thin line between designed absence and negligence. If you step back without building the floor first, you aren't developing people; you're abandoning them. Designed absence takes months of preparation so that vacating the chair is the final step, not the only step.

When it works, it produces a very specific kind of success: quiet success. And quiet success creates two new problems I didn't see coming.

## Problem one: outward legibility

Six months ago, a quiet week on my teams meant something was wrong. A 1:1 without an escalation meant someone was stuck and hiding it. A smooth standup meant the real blockers weren't being named.

By midsummer, the signal had flipped. Silence meant the structure was holding.

To people who had watched that transition happen over six months, the calm read as maturity. To a new VP one week into the role, with zero accumulated context and a mandate to assess the division, the exact same silence looked like a data void.

My working model had a flawed assumption baked in: that good outcomes are self-evident. They aren't. Visibility is constructed for a specific reader.

I teach this exact rule when coaching engineers on technical documentation: who is the reader, what do they already know, and what do they need to extract? An estimation breakdown for a project manager and a systems analysis for a new senior engineer are different documents. Somehow I had spent years preaching audience-first writing and never applied it to how my own teams broadcast their work.

The fix wasn't for me to start writing weekly status digests. Mandated top-down reporting creates compliance, not ownership. The fix was coaching my leads to treat outward legibility as part of running an autonomous team. A week and a half after that conversation with my VP, one of my leads sent an unprompted team status update framing their delivery and risks clearly for leadership; that same afternoon, another squad shipped a clean release with zero fanfare. Same autonomy, newly legible.

## Problem two: operational autonomy vs direction

In August, our goal-tracking tool sent me an automated reminder that I hadn't logged in for fifteen days. During those fifteen days, 359 tasks and milestones had moved across my three teams without me touching a single one.

Two weeks later, on a normal Tuesday, I checked our GitHub activity log. Across the sprint cycle, the three teams had merged 25 pull requests and closed 50 tasks. Zero of them involved me.

I sent only one Slack message that entire day: "Good morning, can someone remind me: has our OKR tool training happened yet?"

That single message bothered me more than any escalation would have. Not because the training had slipped, but because I was the only person asking about it.

Operational autonomy and directional autonomy are two different layers. My teams have operational autonomy down cold: they run standups, ship features, handle production trade-offs, and cover each other's holidays without looking upstairs.

Directional autonomy is knowing whether all that motion is moving the right metric, and caring enough to check the scoreboard without being prompted. When teams merge 25 PRs in a day and the director is still the only person asking whether anyone set up the goal tracker, you have self-sufficient execution paired with outsourced direction.

## What the silence means now

When your teams finally stand on their own feet, your job doesn't disappear. It changes altitude.

You stop listening for whether the daily delivery is happening. Instead, you become responsible for two things the teams won't automatically build for themselves: making sure their quiet competence is legible to the rest of the organisation, and pushing ownership down one more layer, from "we can ship the roadmap without you" to "we know whether the roadmap is working."

The silence has changed meaning. I'm still recalibrating my ears to read it.
